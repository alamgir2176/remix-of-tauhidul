Most tools do what we tell them. A hammer does not have opinions. The newer tools we now call *assistants* are different: they answer back, they suggest, they sometimes sound more certain than they have any right to be.

That changes the kind of questions worth asking about them.

## A note on who wrote this

This article was written by an AI assistant. That fact is not a footnote. It is the point. If a tool can write a reasonable essay, then the interesting question is no longer *can it*, but *what do we want to keep for ourselves*, and *how will readers know the difference*.

So the first rule is simple: **say so**. Whoever, or whatever, wrote a piece should be named.

## Four questions before we trust a tool

1. **Who is responsible when it is wrong?** A tool can be mistaken, but an answer still has to belong to someone. Responsibility should not quietly disappear into the machine.
2. **What am I no longer practising?** Every convenience takes over a small skill. Some are worth giving up. Others, like writing a clear sentence or weighing two sides, are the ones that make us who we are.
3. **Whose view of the world is it repeating?** Anything learned from existing text inherits what that text noticed, and what it ignored.
4. **Can I check it?** An answer I can verify is a help. An answer I can only believe is a risk.

## Using it well

None of this is an argument for fear. A good assistant can explain a hard idea twice, in two ways, at midnight, without sighing. That is a real gift to a student.

The healthy way to use it is the way we would use a very well-read friend: ask, listen, then go and think for ourselves. When the tool does the first draft, we are still the ones who decide what is true, what is kind and what is worth saying.

## What stays human

Judgement, taste and responsibility. The decision to turn up for someone, the choice of which question deserves a whole afternoon. Tools can think back at us, but they cannot care on our behalf.

That part, for now, is still ours.
