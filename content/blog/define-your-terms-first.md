Most arguments I have seen do not fail because one side is wrong. They fail because the two sides are using the same word to mean different things, and neither has noticed.

Take a word like *fair*. One person means "everyone gets the same". Another means "everyone gets what they need". Both are sincere, both are reasonable, and they can talk for an hour without moving, because they are not disagreeing about the world. They are disagreeing about a definition.

## The habit

Studying philosophy trains one small habit above the rest: before arguing, stop and ask what the key words mean. It sounds fussy. In practice it is the fastest way to find out whether there is a real disagreement at all.

It works in three steps:

1. **Name the word doing the heavy lifting.** In "the new rule is unfair", the word is *unfair*.
2. **Say what you mean by it, in plain language.** "I mean it treats people in the same situation differently."
3. **Ask the other person to do the same.** Not as a trap, but as an invitation.

Often the argument dissolves. Sometimes it becomes sharper and more interesting, because now both people can see exactly where they differ.

## Why plain words matter

A definition that only works in jargon is usually not finished. If an idea cannot be explained to a curious friend who has never studied the subject, there is a good chance the idea itself is still a little blurry. Plain words are not a simplification of thinking. They are a test of it.

> Clear writing is what is left when the confusion has been taken out.

That is also why this habit is kind. When we define our terms, we make it easier for the other person to follow us, to correct us, and to agree with us if they can.

## A small practice

Next time you are in a discussion that is going in circles, try one question: *"When you say that, what do you mean exactly?"* Ask it gently, and be ready to answer it yourself.

You may find the circle was never about the topic. It was about a word.
